The benchmark run

Everything on this site except the coverage page comes from one experiment, qfo_region_midi_plus, a single benchmark run. It searched 998 human proteins against the proteomes of 15 relatives from the Quest for Orthologs reference proteomes, release 2020_04. Most of the human proteins are encoded on chromosome 6; the rest are immune proteins (KIR and LILR receptors, CD1 and MR1, and β2-microglobulin). The copied tables do not record how many are in each group. The run finished on 2026-09-13 on Stanford's Sherlock cluster. It has 205,653 of the 3,975,483 rows of results.parquet, one per score. The leaderboard groups them into 29,379 combinations of tool, setting, relative, truth set, raw or dedup, and subset. Its cost is in compute.parquet. Both tables were built by make leaderboard-tables in seanome/2024-kmerseek-analysis at commit 4a4b1171. These 998 proteins are not a random sample of the human proteome.

No tool wins everywhere

No tool wins everywhere. So this board ranks tools within a subset of the proteins (by relative, identity, region length, disorder or feature type), and gives no overall score. Two earlier community benchmarks found the same. The Quest for Orthologs benchmark ran 15 orthology methods on 20 benchmarks. Among the balanced methods, "no method consistently obtained a better balance than the other methods across all data sets". Its authors chose not to compute a combined score, because the right trade-off depends on the application (Altenhoff et al. 2016). The Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB), for text embedding models, reported that "no particular text embedding method dominates across all tasks" (Muennighoff et al. 2023).

In Proceedings of the 17th Conference of the European Chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics, 2014–37. Dubrovnik, Croatia, 2023. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2023.eacl-main.148.

From a search hit to a call

Each tool reports aligned regions: a stretch of the human protein matched to a stretch of a protein in the relative. A region becomes a call when it covers at least half of an annotated domain or feature on the relative's protein. The call names that annotation and sits on the human protein where the region is. A call is correct when the human protein carries that annotation and the call overlaps it with an IoU of at least 0.5. IoU (intersection over union) is the number of residues the call and the true region share, divided by the number of residues covered by either:

\[\text{IoU} = \frac{|\text{call} \cap \text{true region}|}{|\text{call} \cup \text{true region}|}\]

For example, a call on residues 30–129 and a true domain on residues 10–109 share 80 residues (30–109). Together they cover 120 (10–129). So IoU = 80 / 120 = 0.67, and the call counts as correct. A call of the right family that misses the region counts as wrong. The IoU is drawn as bars on a protein line, with ends you can move, on How the scores work.

A true annotation is reachable when its family occurs somewhere in the relative's proteome. A family the relative lacks cannot be copied from it by any tool.

Real examples

The scores

Fmax

Fmax is the best balance of precision and recall a tool reaches at any score cutoff, counted protein by protein as in the CAFA function-prediction challenge. At a cutoff \(t\), the precision of protein \(i\) is its correct calls divided by all its calls scoring at least \(t\). Its recall is the true annotations found divided by its true annotations. Precision is averaged over the \(m(t)\) proteins with at least one call at or above \(t\). Recall is averaged over all \(n\) proteins with an annotation, and counts every true annotation, whether or not the relative could supply it:

\[\text{Pr}(t) = \frac{1}{m(t)}\sum_{i} \frac{\text{correct}_i(t)}{\text{calls}_i(t)}\]

\[\text{Rc}(t) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i} \frac{\text{found}_i(t)}{\text{true}_i}\]

\[F_{\max} = \max_t \frac{2\,\text{Pr}(t)\,\text{Rc}(t)}{\text{Pr}(t)+\text{Rc}(t)}\]

The cutoffs tried are 101 evenly spaced quantiles of the tool's call scores. Worked example: phmmer searching zebrafish, Pfam truth set, raw calls, all proteins. At the best cutoff (bit score on the tool's bit score) precision is 0.2471 and recall is 0.5108. So F = 2 × 0.2471 × 0.5108 / (0.2471 + 0.5108) = 0.3331, the Fmax in Table 1.

AUPRC

AUPRC, the area under the precision-recall curve, sums precision over every cutoff, weighted by the recall gained at that cutoff:

\[\text{AUPRC} = \sum_k (R_k - R_{k-1})\,P_k\]

Unlike Fmax, AUPRC pools all proteins. \(P_k\) is correct calls ÷ all judged calls at cutoff \(k\). \(R_k\) is reachable annotations found ÷ all reachable annotations. A tool that makes many calls per protein pays for each wrong one. A call in a part of a protein that no annotation covers is left out of \(P_k\), since nobody has looked there; Fmax counts it. Worked example, same row: AUPRC = 0.0291. The curve itself is in the source run (all_domain_curves.parquet), not in these tables, so the sum cannot be recomputed here.

Median IoU

Median IoU is the middle IoU of all correct calls, with single-residue annotations left out (an IoU against one residue says nothing about where the ends are). Worked example, same row: 0.700, so half of phmmer's correct calls on zebrafish overlap their true domain by at least that much. A correct call on a region longer than one residue has an IoU of at least 0.5. So the run stores 0 only when there is no such call. Either there are no correct calls at all, or the correct calls are on single residues only (the 1 aa length bin and the ACT_SITE, BINDING and SITE features). The pages show the first case as no correct calls and the second as correct calls only on single residues, never as 0. They tell the two apart by Fmax: an Fmax above 0 means at least one call was correct.

True instances

True instances (n) is the number of annotations on the human proteins in the subset, reachable or not. Same row: 2,435.

Core-hours per 1,000 queries

Core-hours are taken from the run's Nextflow task records: the cores a task reserved multiplied by its wall time, so a task that sat partly idle still counts all its cores. The tables record them per tool and setting, summed over every relative searched, with building the databases as separate rows. The leaderboard divides the search's core-hours by the number of relatives searched and by the 998 queries, times 1,000: the mean cost of searching one relative. Worked example: phmmer reserved 8 cores for 2,806 s over its 15 searches. That is 8 × 2,806 / 3,600 = 6.2356 core-hours, and 6.2356 / 15 × 1,000 / 998 = 0.4165 core-hours per 1,000 queries per relative. Peak RAM is the largest resident memory of any of those tasks. The tool cards give the database builds separately.

Numbers marked measured come from these records, on Sherlock (partition hns). No search used a GPU. ProstT5 has to turn sequences into 3Di shape letters before it can search; that step ran on one GPU as part of building its databases, queries included, and is on the ProstT5 card (0.664 GPU-hours over 3 databases), not in the per-query cost. Numbers marked reported would come from a tool's submitter, on hardware they name; none have been submitted yet.

Estimated cost

\[\text{cost\_usd\_est} = \text{CPU-hours} \times r_\text{cpu} + \text{GPU-hours} \times r_\text{gpu}\]

per 1,000 queries. The default \(r_\text{cpu}\) = $0.044625 per CPU-hour is half of AWS's on-demand price for a c7i.large (2 vCPU, $0.08925 per hour). The default \(r_\text{gpu}\) = $1.006 per GPU-hour is a g5.xlarge (one NVIDIA A10G). Both are for US East (N. Virginia), Linux, from the EC2 on-demand price page (price file, published 2026-10-12, read 2026-10-15). A core-hour is priced as one CPU-hour. Memory is not priced. Worked example: 0.4165 × 0.044625 + 0 × 1.006 = $0.01859 for phmmer against zebrafish. Change either price on the leaderboard page.

Truth sets

Pfam

Every Pfam-A domain with coordinates on the human proteins, from the Pfam regions file for UniProt reference proteomes. The same file supplies the domains on each relative's proteins, which are what a call copies. Pfam families are defined by profile models, so tools that build profiles (jackhmmer, HH-suite) are graded against the kind of model they build, and hmmscan against its own models. Release of the regions file: not recorded in the run; the hmmscan models are Pfam-A 38.2. hmmscan was scored on raw calls only, so it shows "not run" under dedup.

Pfam-N

Pfam-N is an extension of Pfam made by a deep-learning model: it labels regions the Pfam profile models missed. It is only published for Pfam 35.0. This is the one truth set the profile models did not produce. Its regions were filtered to the human proteins queried here. Few of them can be copied from any relative: against zebrafish, 44,246 of the 44,315 Pfam-N regions have no domain of the same family in the zebrafish proteome. So every tool scores low on Pfam-N, and the set says little about which tool is better.

Swiss-Prot features

Curated features from Swiss-Prot entries, of 12 types (the feature keys on the leaderboard, such as TRANSMEM for a membrane-spanning segment and ACT_SITE for an active site residue). They come from the literature, not from any sequence or structure model. Single-residue features (ACT_SITE, BINDING, SITE) are scored by whether the call covers the residue, since an IoU against one residue cannot reach 0.5. A Swiss-Prot feature counts as reachable when the relative has a protein that carries the same feature type and shares a Pfam family with the human protein.

Dedup against raw

Raw keeps every call a tool makes. One human region that matches 300 mouse kinases becomes 300 kinase calls on nearly the same residues, and all but one count as wrong. Dedup merges calls of the same family on the same human protein that overlap each other with an IoU of 0.5 or more, keeping the highest-scoring one. Two copies of a domain side by side barely overlap, so both are kept. Comparing the two separates finding a domain from reporting it many times. For phmmer on zebrafish (Pfam, all proteins): Fmax 0.333 raw and 0.457 dedup. The same calls drawn raw and after dedup, on a made-up protein: How the scores work.

Subsets of the proteins

The tables call each subset a stratum. There are four ways to cut the proteins:

  • Nearest reviewed relative, % identity: for each human domain, the percent identity to the closest domain of the same family in the relative. Bins: 0–20, 20–30, 30–40, 40–60 and 60–100%. A domain whose family is absent from the relative is "no same-family domain".
  • Nearest reviewed relative, Mya: the relative searched, labelled with how many million years ago its line split from ours.
  • Region type and length: the length of the annotated region, in residues. Bins up to 60 aa are short features; 61 aa and up is the size of a folded domain. Whole-protein annotations were not scored in this run.
  • Disorder: the mean AlphaFold confidence (pLDDT, 0–100) of the human protein. Low pLDDT marks regions without a fixed shape.
  • Feature type: the Swiss-Prot feature key, for the Swiss-Prot truth set only.

The run computed each subset on its own, so two subsets cannot be combined; a combination with no row shows × and "not run".

No-search baselines

A baseline row reads only the query sequence: it searches no relative and copies nothing. It answers what score a reader gets with no database at all. Gillis and Pavlidis (2013) asked every function benchmark for such a row, after a method that gave every protein the same annotation scored well in the first CAFA. Each baseline is scored by the same code and against the same truth tables as the search tools.

  • Kyte-Doolittle scan: Hydropathy (Kyte and Doolittle 1982) averaged over 19-residue windows; windows above 1.6 are called membrane-spanning. The scan of notebook 231. Can address: Swiss-Prot TRANSMEM and INTRAMEM features. Swiss-Prot features, INTRAMEM: Fmax 0.333 (n = 9, fewer than 30: too few to read); Swiss-Prot features, TRANSMEM: Fmax 0.778 (n = 1,124).
  • IUPred long: IUPred 1.0, long mode; runs of 20 or more residues scoring above 0.5 are called disordered. Can address: Swiss-Prot REGION features (all, and those noted Disordered) and COMPBIAS. Not in this build's table yet.
  • metapredict: metapredict 3.0.1; runs of 20 or more residues scoring above 0.5 are called disordered. Can address: Swiss-Prot REGION features (all, and those noted Disordered) and COMPBIAS. Not in this build's table yet.
  • SEG: segmasker from BLAST+ 2.16.0 at its default settings (window 12, locut 2.2, hicut 2.5); each masked stretch is a call. Can address: Swiss-Prot COMPBIAS and REPEAT features. Not in this build's table yet.
  • Pfam family frequency: Every query gets, over its whole length, the Pfam family held by the most human proteins of its length, the queries themselves left out. Can address: Pfam and Pfam-N. Pfam, all proteins: Fmax 0.035 (n = 2,435).

Each baseline can address only some cells: a hydropathy scan can call a membrane-spanning segment but not a Pfam family. Every other cell shows not applicable, never 0. In Table 1 the baselines sit under the search tools, whatever the sort.

Reading a tool below a baseline. Where a search tool's Fmax is below a baseline's on the same truth set and subset, the search has found nothing there that the query sequence alone does not give. On Swiss-Prot TRANSMEM, for example, a tool below the Kyte-Doolittle scan calls membrane-spanning segments less well than a hydropathy average does, so its score there says nothing about whether it found related proteins. On Pfam, a tool below the Pfam family frequency row does no better than giving every protein the most common family of its length.

A baseline row is the same under every relative and under raw and dedup, since it searches no relative and makes no copies to merge. Its cost is the run time of its own tasks per 1,000 queries, from the run's task records; where the table holds none, the cost reads not measured. Source of these rows: laptop check: tables/no_search_baselines_laptop_check (committed code, midi-plus truth tables copied from Sherlock).

SCOPe40: ranking a domain's relatives

Scored for two tools so far, on the SCOPe40 page. This section gives the rules. The question: given one known domain, does the tool rank the domain's known relatives above the first unrelated hit? Here a relative is a domain of the same family, superfamily or fold, one level at a time; an unrelated hit is a domain of a different fold. The Foldseek (a structure search tool) and MMseqs2 (a sequence search tool) numbers are read from per-query files that other authors published.

SCOPe (Structural Classification of Proteins, extended) sorts protein domains into four nested levels. An id such as b.1.1.1 reads class.fold.superfamily.family: class b (all beta), fold 1, superfamily 1, family 1. Domains in one superfamily are taken to share an ancestor. Domains in one fold share a shape, with or without an ancestor. A family numbered 0 holds SCOPe's automated matches: domains placed in the superfamily but in no curated family. Each domain has its own id, such as d6jc0b_: d, the PDB entry (6jc0), the chain (b) and a domain number (_ for the whole chain). SCOPe40 is the set of SCOPe domains with no two above 40% sequence identity, as distributed by ASTRAL, SCOPe's companion set of domain sequences and structures (Chandonia et al. 2022).

What Foldseek's paper counts

The paper that set this benchmark, van Kempen et al. (2024), defines the labels in its Methods (quoted under its CC BY 4.0 licence):

"Per domain, we measured the fraction of detected TPs up to the first FP. For family-level, superfamily-level and fold-level recognition, TPs were defined as same family, same superfamily and not same family and same fold and not same superfamily, respectively. Hits from different folds are FPs."
"After sorting the alignment result of each query (...), we calculated the sensitivity as the fraction of TPs in the sorted list up to the first FP, all excluding self-hits. For comparison, we took the mean sensitivity over all queries for family-level, superfamily-level and fold-level classifications. We evaluated only SCOPe members with at least one other family, superfamily and fold member."

Its main text gives the denominator: "the fraction of TPs out of all possible correct matches until the first FP, a match to a different fold". Its Figure 2a draws the per-query values as a cumulative curve and says "Sensitivity is the area under the ROC (AUROC) curve up to the first FP". A ROC curve for one query plots TPs found against FPs found. Cut at the first FP, its area is the share of TPs found above the cut, which is the sensitivity defined below. The paper's numbers come from a scoring script in Foldseek's analysis repository, bench.noselfhit.awk.

For one query, a true positive (TP) is a hit at the level being scored. A false positive (FP) is a hit in a different fold. Any other hit is not counted: neither TP nor FP. Sort the hits by the tool's own score, best first, after dropping the query's hit to itself. Walk down the list and stop at the first FP. The sensitivity up to the first false positive is the number of TPs above that cut divided by the number of possible TPs for that query in the whole set. The walk uses every hit the tool reports at the settings it was run with.

This site first planned a nested rule. A hit is a superfamily TP when query and target share the first three fields of the SCOPe id (class letter, fold, superfamily), family included. It is an FP when their folds differ, and not counted when they share the fold only. Figure 18, on the SCOPe40 page, labels one made-up hit list both ways.

Over many queries, the area under the curve (AUC) sorts the queries by sensitivity, highest first, and plots sensitivity against the fraction of queries. Its area is the mean sensitivity over all queries, so the AUC and the mean are the same number. Coverage is the share of queries with at least one hit that passes the tool's default cutoff; it goes next to every AUC.

Every query counts. A query that gets no hits scores 0 and stays in every mean and every area. The one query that cannot count at a level is one with no possible TP there: its sensitivity would be 0 of 0. kmerseek is the k-mer search tool whose settings fill most rows of this site. An earlier kmerseek claim, that it "outperforms Foldseek on SCOPe", computed the area under the curve over only the queries kmerseek returned hits for, 3.3% of them. That claim and its number are retired. In its source, notebook 076, kmerseek detected 188 of the 5,713 queries of Foldseek's SCOPe 2.08 file (3.3%) and took its mean over the 228 queries both tools scored.

The paper's Figure 2a legend reads "TPs are matches within the same superfamily", which is the nested rule; its Methods, quoted above, and its script leave the family out. A third rule, in the Methods subsection that fits Foldseek's probability of a true match, ignores "hits to the same family or to another superfamily".

Where the extra 1 comes from: the script counts the possible family TPs as the family size minus 1, which removes the query itself. For the superfamily it takes the superfamily size minus (the family size minus 1), and for the fold the fold size minus (the superfamily size minus 1). Those two leave the query in, so each is one more than the number of domains that could be found. In the script: lines 7, 10 and 13 count every domain, the query included, into its family, superfamily and fold; line 17 drops the self-hit from the hit list; line 25 takes 1 off the family count, but lines 26 and 27 subtract only the other family members (or the other superfamily members), so the query stays in. It can never be found at those levels: its hit to itself is dropped, and would count at the family level anyway. So no query can reach a superfamily or fold sensitivity of 1.

The extra 1 is in the per-query files of Foldseek and MMseqs2 on SCOPe 2.08 that other authors published, as copied into seanome/2024-kmerseek-analysis (data/tea_scope40_rocx_files). Query d6jc0b_ (d.41.5.0) has 6 domains in its superfamily and 5 in its family, itself included in both, so 1 possible superfamily TP. Foldseek found it and the file says 0.5: 1 of 2. Over the 5,713 queries of the Foldseek file, the mean superfamily sensitivity is 0.574 as published and 0.641 over the possible TPs; at the fold level 0.102 and 0.111. For MMseqs2: superfamily 0.172 and 0.205, fold 0.001 and 0.001. The family level has no extra 1 (0.817 for Foldseek either way). A comparison with these files, or with the paper's own numbers, has to use the script's denominators, or rescore Foldseek.

At the fold level, a TP is not always a relative: two domains can share a fold with no shared ancestor (Cheng et al. 2008). So a fold-level TP means "same shape", not "homolog".

The domain set

Foldseek's set is SCOPe 2.01 at 40% identity, 11,211 domains (Foldseek's list scop_lookup.fix.tsv, as copied into seanome/2024-kmerseek-analysis). Choices made (Olga, 2026-10-17)

  1. The rule: the paper's Methods and script. Its Figure 2a legend reads differently (above).
  2. The denominator: the script's, one more than the domains that can be found at the superfamily and fold levels, so the numbers match the published files. The other is kept beside it in the long table (sensitivity_possible).
  3. The queries: Foldseek's set, domains with another member at all three levels, computed on SCOPe 2.08: 5,737.
  4. The set: SCOPe 2.08 at 40% identity, 15,177 domains, searched all against all.
  5. Hit lists: still missing for every tool. The two scored rows come from per-query files with no hit lists; the pipeline that keeps them for every tool is written, not launched.

The score each tool is ranked by

Each tool's hits are sorted by its own score, best first, ties broken by target id. In the pipeline: Foldseek, ProstT5 + Foldseek, MMseqs2 (both modes), phmmer, jackhmmer and HH-suite by bit score, higher first; kmerseek by region E-value, smaller first. The two published files do not record the order their runs used.
